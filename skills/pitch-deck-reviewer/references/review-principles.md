# Generalized pitch review principles

Apply these principles to the actual deck. They synthesize recurring review patterns without reproducing private companies, people, metrics, or case studies. Treat them as questions to resolve, not a rigid slide template.

## Customer and problem

Identify the user, buyer, and budget owner; they may differ. Specify the initial segment, geography, and any prerequisite that narrows eligibility. Replace a general industry description with a concrete recurring problem and its consequences: time, money, missed decisions, operational disruption, or customer frustration. Assess whether the evidence comes from the intended customer.

## Solution and product reality

Explain what the product is, how a customer accesses it, what they do, and the outcome. Separate the benefit from the mechanism. Use one readable workflow, screenshot, or before-and-after example instead of a dense inventory of features. Clarify prototype, pilot, and live status. For technical products, explain the practical output before introducing the detailed science or architecture. Explain AI's specific role and the evidence for improvement.

## Story and sequence

Introduce concepts before using their labels. Define necessary acronyms. Group related modules so multiple product names do not obscure the core proposition. Put the strongest relevant evidence early once the reader can understand it. Move deep architecture, long competitor tables, and detailed budgets to the appendix when they interrupt the story. Use slide headlines to convey takeaways. End with a clear decision, memorable vision, or both, suited to the audience.

## Evidence and traction

Distinguish product traction from consulting work, a legacy business, internal testing, and the founder's existing audience. Label prototype use, pilots, signed commitments, paying accounts, and retained customers accurately. Define "customer," "user," "lead," "partner," and "active." Include dates and denominators where meaningful. Explain what company logos represent. Show the outcome and repeat behavior, not merely activity. Do not assume an audience converts to demand or that synthetic personas establish real customer validation.

## Market and timing

Start with the reachable customer and expansion path. Explain TAM, SAM, and SOM assumptions, including eligible customers, geography, price, and unit of purchase. Separate software spending from underlying industry spending and a long-term revenue target. Ask for traceable sources and dates. Explain why a market change matters to this customer now, rather than adding context without consequence.

## Pricing and business model

State who pays, for what, and how often. Explain the unit: organization, seat, transaction, usage, or another basis. Distinguish subscription revenue, transaction volume, pass-through spending, and savings. Reconcile monthly/annual pricing, customer counts, conversion assumptions, average revenue, and forecast revenue. Where relevant, show the operating or production costs needed to deliver the product. A projection is an assumption to support, not evidence of achieved revenue.

## Competition and advantage

Include the current workaround as well as relevant vendors. Compare on buying criteria that matter to the first customer. Avoid arbitrary axes, unexplained superlatives, or a feature matrix that declares every product equivalent. Explain why the customer switches, the work required to switch, and the supported advantage. Do not claim a durable moat merely because AI, integration, patents, or network effects are mentioned.

## Acquisition and growth

Describe where the first customers come from, who sells, how purchase and onboarding happen, and how the motion can repeat. Explain the mechanism behind referrals or network effects and whether it has been observed. For a marketplace, ask how each side obtains value before broad participation. For a channel strategy, clarify the partner's role and commitment. Connect customer-growth forecasts to actual acquisition assumptions.

## Team and execution

Show relevant experience, insight, access, and execution evidence. Explain why this team can deliver this specific product. Separate current leaders, contractors, advisors, committed support, and planned hires. Advisor volume is not a substitute for relevant contribution. For technical or physical products, examine whether the pitch explains development status, manufacturing scale, infrastructure, and capital requirements.

## Ask and milestones

State the decision being requested, amount, use, time period, and milestones. Distinguish historical spending, committed funding, and incremental requests. Explain what commercial or technical uncertainty the next stage resolves. Use measurable progress appropriate to the stage instead of treating a list of features as the entire milestone plan. Do not give legal or cap-table advice.

## Corporate venture distinctions

Describe strategic fit in concrete terms: customer access, internal use, distribution, capabilities, or economic benefit. Separate the parent's scale from external willingness to pay. Label modeled efficiency gains and explain adoption, implementation, integration, and transition assumptions. Identify the accountable operator and the evidence required for further funding. A useful internal tool and a scalable external product can have different cases for investment.

## Business type distinctions

Identify the kind of business and the kind of raise before applying the sections above. Most of this guidance assumes venture-backed software. Adjust when the deck is something else, and do not hold a friends-and-family round, a small angel round, project financing, or a public listing to venture expectations on growth or exit.

**Consumer products.** Separate the number of stores stocking the product from the rate it sells in each store. Ask for sales per store per week or month, reorders by retailers, and repeat purchase by online customers. Distinguish selling into a retailer or distributor from selling through to shoppers. Check gross margin by channel after distributor margin, retailer margin, promotions, and shipping, and whether the blended margin supports any profit claim. Ask who manufactures the product, at what capacity, and how exposed the cost is to a key ingredient. Inventory and receivables consume cash as sales grow; ask how the round funds them. Expect a product slide with price, format, and what is in it. Health, nutrition, and performance claims may be regulated. Flag them for verification instead of judging them.

**Capital-heavy businesses** (manufacturing, hardware, energy, deep technology). State the readiness plainly: lab result, pilot, demonstration plant, or commercial production, and at what volume. Ask what one facility or unit costs, how long it takes to build, and how it is financed across equity, debt, grants, and partners. An early round rarely pays for the plant; ask what it proves so that the larger financing becomes available. Treat the supply of inputs as seriously as demand: source, contract, price, and volume. For purchase agreements, distinguish binding contracts from letters of intent, and ask for volume, price, term, and whether the buyer has tested the product. Compare projected economics at full capacity with the ramp-up period, and with a conventional operator run for the same hours. Separate patents granted from patents applied for, and ask about permits.

**Funds and investment vehicles** (asset managers, yield strategies, holding companies, public-company financings). Establish what the investor would own: units in a fund, shares in a management company, or shares in a company that holds the assets. These are different pitches. For a track record, ask for the period, the capital it was earned on and whose capital it was, whether returns are after fees and costs, who verified them, the worst loss, and a relevant benchmark. Ask how much capital the strategy can absorb before returns fall. State the fee or revenue model and current assets under management. Expect a plain risk section covering counterparties, custody, liquidity, leverage, and regulation, and check that it does not contradict safety language elsewhere in the deck. Check that disclaimers describe the vehicle actually being offered, and refer legal wording to counsel. Review how the record is presented. Do not judge whether the returns are attractive or whether to invest.

## Visual communication

Judge actual readability: hierarchy, text density, contrast, screenshot size, chart labels, spacing, consistency, and clipping. Favor a clear takeaway and a readable example over decorative complexity. Preserve useful detail in the appendix. Recommend a specific change and name the communication problem first. When the whole deck needs polish, also suggest hiring a designer; never let that suggestion replace naming the problem. Match visual claims to what was actually inspected.
