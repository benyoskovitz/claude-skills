# Slide checklists

Use these questions slide by slide. A deck does not need every slide type, and the order can vary. When a slide passes, move on; when it fails, explain which question it fails and what to change.

## Title and introduction
- Is the company name obvious and written out, not only a stylized logo?
- Does one line say what the company does and for whom, so the category is clear at once?
- Does the customer benefit lead, rather than a mechanism or a percentage?
- Are the founder's name and email present?
- Red flags: dates, event names, disclaimers, hashtags, navigation bars, decorative or confusing images, acronyms, a tiny screenshot.

## Problem
- Who has this problem, specifically?
- Can the reader feel the pain? What does it cost: money, time, failure, risk?
- Is this a real problem or just a description of a market?
- Do existing tools already solve it? If so, what's still broken?
- What changes when it's fixed?
- If the point is already widely accepted, is it stated in one line before moving on?
- Does the reader need context first, such as what this thing is and why it matters?
- Red flags: ambiguous wording, the target customer revealed only later, over-arguing an accepted trend, unsourced statistics, unattributed quotes, a headline that reads as if the company causes the problem.

## Solution, product, and how it works
- Is the solution framed as the answer to the specific problems named?
- Can the reader see it: screens, a flow, a diagram, one example walked through?
- What does the user actually do, step by step?
- What happens in the edge cases?
- Is the value clear, or only the mechanics?
- Is there one lead product or use case?
- Is the technology, including any AI, explained as what it does, on one slide?
- Red flags: links instead of content, too many product names, screenshots too small to read, feature lists, technology without the product experience.

## Why now
- Is there a reason this is urgent today: technology readiness, a shift in demand, regulation, timing?
- Does it sit right after the solution or traction, where it adds urgency?
- For a regional focus, why this region now?

## Market size
- Are the assumptions grounded and sourced, ideally from the total market down to the reachable market and the share you can win?
- Is it today's size with a growth rate, not a far-future number?
- Does it look stretched to justify a big number, or oddly small?
- Is the company clearly placed in any market chart or diagram?
- Red flags: a tiny share-of-market statistic, stretched user counts, the market slide buried in the appendix, two market slides saying the same thing.

## Business model and pricing
- How does the company make money, and does the reader learn this early enough?
- What does it charge, and compared to what?
- Is there one clear pricing frame, the standard one for the category?
- Does the model fit the value proposition?
- Are unit economics or a return example shown?
- Who pays, and can they afford the margin assumed?

## Traction
- What stage is the company at?
- What exactly does each number mean, and is it current?
- How does it connect to revenue?
- Are logos labelled as customers, partners, letters of intent, or supporters?
- Is there one case study that tells the story?
- Is it early enough in the deck to support the claims and the ask?
- With no traction yet: is there a waitlist, letters of intent, pre-sales, or pilot interest?
- Red flags: activity lists, internal testing, outreach counts, old dates, vanity metrics, unit economics that look too good, hype that outruns the proof.

## Competition
- Are the obvious alternatives the reader already has in mind included, including the current workaround?
- Why is the company much better, not slightly better?
- Is there context per competitor: stage, geography, difference?
- Are the axes meaningful and labelled?
- Is the wedge stated explicitly?
- Red flags: generic images instead of real companies, unsupported first-mover claims, placing a well-known competitor where readers will disagree.

## Go-to-market
- Who does the company sell to first, and through what: direct sales, platforms, partners?
- What's the growth engine, and what evidence supports it?
- Who are the named partners and what do they do?
- What are the goals for the next 12 months?
- What does acquiring a customer cost, or what is the plan to find out?

## Team
- Why is this team uniquely qualified?
- Is the founder's relevant background explicit and early?
- Are there real photos, clear bios, readable formatting?
- Is a solo founder framed honestly?
- Are advisors relevant and few?
- Red flags: names and titles only, friends or family as advisors, irrelevant details, a QR code instead of a story.

## Financials and projections
- Are bottom-up assumptions shown?
- Do the numbers reconcile with pricing and customer counts?
- Are revenue and profit both shown, with a clear timing basis and a sensible chart scale?
- Does the growth look venture-scale?

## Ask and use of funds
- How much, for what, which milestones, and how much runway?
- Does the amount fit the plan?
- Will the company need to raise again soon, and is that consistent with the rest of the deck?
- Is the structure clear, and does anything look like an obvious red flag? Flag it without giving valuation or term advice.
- Red flags: past rounds and share prices, contingency lines, an ask that comes before the team.

## Roadmap and timeline
- Does it show 12 to 24 months of major product and go-to-market milestones, simply laid out?
- Is history trimmed to the recent past, without anything that hurts, such as declined pilots?

## Vision and close
- Is the mission or vision the last impression, with contact details?
- If the vision slide is mid-deck, should it move to the end?
- Is there an exit slide at an early stage, or a filler closing slide? Cut it.

## Appendix
- Dense specs, technical detail, disclaimers, and detailed screenshots belong here.
- Does a useful appendix slide belong in the main flow instead?

## Design and visuals, across the deck
- Does it look professional, especially if the business is design or media related?
- Are fonts, spacing, punctuation, and alignment consistent?
- Do visuals explain, rather than decorate?
- Is the deck in one language?

## Slides to suggest adding
- A "How it works" slide right after the solution, with three to five features mapped to the pains.
- A founder story slide early, when the founder lived the problem.
- A case study slide.
- A slide on the sector's known risk: regulation, intellectual property, scale and capital, licensing, or operations.
- A pivot slide early, when the company changed direction.
- Section dividers should go, not be added.

## A standard outline

For a deck missing many pieces, propose an outline like this and adapt it to the company: vision, problem, solution, how it works, target market and size, business model and pricing, go-to-market, status and traction, goals for the next 12 to 24 months, why now, competitive advantage, team, ask, close on the vision with contact details.

For a founder who lived the problem, a story arc can work better: the founder's discovery, the problem, why it matters now, the solution, how it works, proof, market, model, go-to-market, team, ask, vision.

## Recommendation moves

Be specific about what to change:
- **Reorder:** give the sequence explicitly, for example "Problem, Solution, Traction, Why now," and say "right after slide N."
- **Split:** name both halves, such as "Roadmap" and "The ask."
- **Merge:** name which slides to combine and what the combined slide leads into.
- **Rewrite:** give the exact replacement headline or line, often two or three options.
- **Add:** name the slide and list its contents.
- **Visuals:** name the visual, such as a process with the broken step marked, a before-and-after cost comparison, or a growth chart.
- **Fill-in sentences:** give the founder a template with bracketed placeholders, such as "Invest [amount], expect [result] over [months]."
- **Cut:** say how much, such as "cut this text by half."
