# Common pitch deck mistakes

The mistakes below come up again and again in real pitch reviews. They are grouped by how often they appear: "very common" means many or most decks have it, "common" means a good number do, "occasional" means some do. Check every deck for the very common ones first.

Each entry says what the mistake looks like, why it hurts, the usual fix, and a line in the review voice. The lines show the voice. Do not repeat them word for word in every review.

All examples here are generic. When you review a deck, describe what you see in that deck, not these examples.

## Very common

### Traction is missing, vague, or disconnected from the story
- **Looks like:** a list of activities instead of results. Numbers with no unit or context (users of what, leads from where, satisfaction among whom). Logos with no label saying whether they are customers, partners, pilots, or supporters. Internal testing or outreach counts presented as traction. Stale figures with an old "as of" date. No traction slide at all.
- **Why it hurts:** investors back proof, not potential. Vague traction raises more questions than it answers and makes every later claim, including the ask and the projections, feel unsupported. Calling testing or emails "traction" costs credibility.
- **Fix:** say exactly what each number means and how it ties to the revenue model. Label logos by relationship. Replace activity lists with one concrete case study: who you worked with, what happened, what you are tracking. Show growth over time. Update stale numbers. With no traction yet, show the nearest signals (waitlist, letters of intent, pre-sales, pilot interest) and say plainly what stage you are at.
- **Voice:** "This is not traction." / "These are good proof points. Make them concrete and connect them to your value proposition."

### The product is told, not shown
- **Looks like:** a solution slide of benefits or technology claims with no screens, no flow, and no walkthrough. The reader can't tell what a user does, step by step. Key mechanics are missing: who does what, when money moves, what happens when something fails. A link to a product page stands in for an explanation.
- **Why it hurts:** without a picture of the product in use, investors can't judge feasibility or difference. They will probe the edge cases, and leaving them out reads as not having thought it through.
- **Fix:** add one or two "How it works" slides right after the solution slide: a user flow, a process diagram, or a few screenshots large enough to read. Walk through one example end to end. Answer the obvious "what if" cases. Never rely on a link; assume no one clicks.
- **Voice:** "Can you show anything? Mockups, a flow, screenshots?" / "Show me the outcome, not the wiring." / "What happens if [the obvious failure case]?"

### Weak title slide and missing contact details
- **Looks like:** a company name that is hard to read from the logo. A category that only becomes clear several slides in. Noise such as dates, event names, disclaimers, hashtags, or a decorative image. No founder name or email on the first or last slide.
- **Why it hurts:** the first slide sets the context for everything after it. Decks get forwarded, and investors need to know who to contact.
- **Fix:** the company name written out, one line that states what the company does and for whom, and the founder's name and email. Remove dates, events, disclaimers, and hashtags. Put contact details on the first and last slides.
- **Voice:** "Make it immediately clear what this is." / "Add your name and email to the first slide."

### Slide order works against the story
- **Looks like:** features before the problem. The problem appearing late. The product or business model referenced before it is explained. Traction buried near the end. Team after the ask. A strong point hidden deep in the deck or in the appendix.
- **Why it hurts:** the reader builds understanding in sequence. Out-of-order slides create confusion and make the ask feel unearned.
- **Fix:** give an explicit sequence. Common moves: context, then problem, then solution; how it works right after the solution; traction right after the solution; why now after the solution or traction; team before the ask; vision last; pull a useful appendix slide into the main flow.
- **Voice:** "Move traction up, right after the solution." / "Put the team before the ask, so you've earned credibility by the time you ask for money."

### Slides to merge, split, or cut
- **Looks like:** thin slides that each carry a fraction of a point. Section-divider slides. A slide that repeats an earlier one. One overloaded slide doing two jobs. Slides that add nothing, such as an old timeline, an irrelevant metric, or an exit slide at an early stage.
- **Why it hurts:** every slide must earn its place. Clutter breaks the story.
- **Fix:** merge thin slides. Remove dividers and put their message in the next slide's headline. Split overloaded slides in two, and name both halves. Cut irrelevant slides.
- **Voice:** "Remove this slide. It's not adding value." / "Break this into two slides."

### The ask is missing or doesn't say what the money buys
- **Looks like:** no ask slide. An amount with no use of funds. Use of funds with no milestones or runway. Too much detail on past rounds and share prices. An amount that doesn't fit the plan. Roadmap and ask crammed onto one slide.
- **Why it hurts:** the ask is a story about what this round unlocks and how it reduces risk for the next one. Without milestones and runway, investors can't judge whether the amount is right.
- **Fix:** state how much, what it's for, which milestones it reaches, and how many months it buys. Cut past-round detail. Check that the amount fits the plan. Split roadmap and ask if they share a slide.
- **Voice:** "How far does this get you?" / "You're asking for money without telling me what it buys."

### Unexplained acronyms, jargon, and insider terms
- **Looks like:** industry acronyms on the first slide. Regulations cited by code. Internal names for tiers or levels. Program or partner names few investors know.
- **Why it hurts:** every unknown term is friction. Readers outside the niche stop reading or misread. An unexplained partner name makes the reader wonder about the relationship.
- **Fix:** spell terms out on first use, add a short definition, or rephrase in plain words. Say who any named partner is and what they do for you.
- **Voice:** "Acronyms = friction." / "Don't assume your reader knows the acronym."

### Typos, grammar, and inconsistent formatting
- **Looks like:** spelling mistakes, missing words, inconsistent punctuation across bullets, font changes mid-slide, misaligned elements, text cut off at the edge, inconsistent number formats.
- **Why it hurts:** small errors chip away at perceived professionalism.
- **Fix:** quote each error and give the correction. Make bullets consistent. Keep this brief, and never make it the headline of the review.
- **Voice:** "Tiny nitpick:" / "Fix typo: [wrong] to [right]."

## Common

### The design needs professional polish
- **Looks like:** plain or inconsistent layouts, a logo or header taking a third of the slide, cramped team bios, inconsistent fonts and spacing.
- **Why it hurts:** a polished deck builds confidence. If the business sells design, media, or messaging, the deck itself is evidence of competence.
- **Fix:** first name the specific problem: hierarchy, density, contrast, spacing, readability. When the whole deck needs polish, also suggest hiring a designer. When the design is good, say so.
- **Voice:** "Consider hiring a designer. It needs to pop." / "Given what your business does, your own deck has to look refined."

### The problem isn't felt
- **Looks like:** a market description instead of a pain. Wording that hides who suffers. Over-arguing a point everyone already accepts. A problem that existing tools seem to solve already. No consequence of doing nothing.
- **Why it hurts:** investors need to feel urgency. If the pain is weak or already handled, the opportunity deflates.
- **Fix:** name who hurts and how badly. Add the cost of the status quo in money, time, downtime, or bad decisions. State what changes when it's fixed. If the trend is accepted, say it in one line and move on to what's broken.
- **Voice:** "Help me feel the pain." / "This isn't a problem. It's a market description." / "What happens when it's fixed?"

### Headlines that don't state the point
- **Looks like:** a topic label instead of a takeaway. A phrase that reads wrong, such as making it sound like the company causes the problem. Hedging phrased as a question. Labels like "Problem (1/2)". A slide with no clear message.
- **Why it hurts:** many readers skim only the headlines. A takeaway headline does the work for them.
- **Fix:** rewrite each headline as the message. Supply the exact rewrite, sometimes two or three options. Drop page labels. Be confident.
- **Voice:** "What's the point of this slide? Add a clear headline and takeaway." / "Be confident. Say it directly."

### The close is weak
- **Looks like:** a bare "Thank you," a closing slide with confusing extra text, no closing slide, or a vision slide stuck in the middle of the deck.
- **Why it hurts:** the last slide is the last impression. Investors should leave inspired and know how to follow up.
- **Fix:** end on the mission or vision plus contact details. Move an existing vision slide to the end. Describe what the world looks like if the company succeeds.
- **Voice:** "End on a high note." / "Leave investors with a sense of what the world looks like if you succeed."

### Unclear target customer, segment, or geography
- **Looks like:** "users" when the deck means "customers." Vague labels such as "institutions" or "SMEs" with no definition. Targeting everyone from tiny companies to enterprises at once. No stated region, so investors assume the biggest, most competitive market.
- **Why it hurts:** segments have different needs, sales motions, and economics. An undefined target makes the go-to-market and the market size hard to believe.
- **Fix:** define the segment by size, industry, and region. Say who you serve today and who you'll serve in 12 to 24 months. State the geography early.
- **Voice:** "Who is your initial target market? Say it clearly and early." / "Be precise: do you mean users or customers?"

### Business model and pricing missing or confusing
- **Looks like:** no pricing. Too many pricing options at once. "Affordable" with no comparison. A revenue model that doesn't match the value proposition. Margin assumptions not checked with whoever pays them.
- **Why it hurts:** investors need to know how this becomes a business before they believe the mission.
- **Fix:** show the model simply, using the standard pricing frame for the category. Compare against what the alternatives cost. Show unit economics or a return example. Check that whoever pays can afford the margin you assume.
- **Voice:** "Affordable compared to what?" / "How does this become a business?"

### Too much text
- **Looks like:** paragraphs, detailed tables or specs in the main flow, screenshots too small to read.
- **Why it hurts:** readers skim. Density hides the signal.
- **Fix:** give a specific cut, such as "cut this by half." Turn paragraphs into short bullets. Keep two or three points per slide. Move detail to the appendix. Pull the two or three key insights onto their own slides.
- **Voice:** "Cut most of this text." / "Ease the reader into the details."

### Too many products, use cases, or segments
- **Looks like:** many product names introduced early. A portfolio that suggests the company does everything. Several use cases with no priority. Positioning that drifts into a different category mid-deck.
- **Why it hurts:** it looks unfocused and raises questions about capacity. Investors won't remember a list of product names.
- **Fix:** pick the wedge, meaning one product, one use case, or one segment to enter first, and lead with it. Show how it expands later. Group products under one platform. Pick one positioning and hold it.
- **Voice:** "If the number one use case is [X], lead with that." / "Make sure the deck doesn't imply you're solving everything for everyone."

### Go-to-market is vague
- **Looks like:** "strategic partnerships" with no names or roles. Growth projections with no engine behind them. Unclear whether you sell direct or through partners.
- **Why it hurts:** projections are only as believable as the engine behind them.
- **Fix:** name the channel and why it works. Explain the growth engine and the evidence for it. Set 12-month goals for pilots, sales, or early revenue. If the fit seems real, sell directly to the most obvious buyer first.
- **Voice:** "Where are these customers coming from?" / "What's the growth engine?"

### Competition without real differentiation
- **Looks like:** a grid that leaves out the alternatives the reader is already thinking of. Axes that don't say what they measure. "First mover" or "proprietary" claims with nothing behind them.
- **Why it hurts:** investors arrive with competitors in mind. If you don't address them, investors assume you can't.
- **Fix:** name the obvious alternatives and say head-on why you are much better, not slightly better. Add a line of context per competitor. Label axes. State your wedge explicitly. Back up first-mover and proprietary claims or drop them.
- **Voice:** "At this point I'm thinking about [obvious alternative]. Address it head-on." / "If that's your wedge, say so."

### The team slide doesn't answer "why you?"
- **Looks like:** names and titles only. The founder's relevant background buried or vague. The team appearing late. A solo founder's slide titled "Team." Stock images instead of photos. A long list of advisors.
- **Why it hurts:** founder-market fit is a core investment question. Advisor lists rarely move investors.
- **Fix:** make the case that this team is uniquely qualified. Add a founder story early if the founder lived the problem. A solo founder should frame the slide around their own edge. Trim advisors to the relevant few.
- **Voice:** "That's a LinkedIn page, not a story." / "Make me believe no one else should be doing this but you."

### Statistics unsourced, unexplained, or off-point
- **Looks like:** a striking claim with no source. A number whose meaning is unclear (is that high or low?). Quotes with no attribution. A share-of-market figure so small it undercuts the story.
- **Why it hurts:** an unsourced number invites doubt. A number without interpretation leaves the reader to decide what it means.
- **Fix:** cite the source. Say what the number means for the story. Choose the statistic with the most weight. Cut the rest.
- **Voice:** "Is that high or low? What's your point?" / "Who is the quote from?"

### No concrete example or case study
- **Looks like:** abstract benefit claims, an aggregate number with no story, a case study whose outcome isn't clear.
- **Why it hurts:** one specific before-and-after makes value tangible in a way a list of benefits never does.
- **Fix:** add a before-and-after use case or a single-customer case study: what was done, what it proved, what success looks like. Offer a fill-in sentence, such as "Worked with [customer] to [do what]; in [time], [result]."
- **Voice:** "Add a real-world example." / "What changed because of it?"

### Projections and numbers that don't add up
- **Looks like:** a large revenue target with no breakdown. Customer counts with no basis. Averages that don't match stated prices. Unit economics that look too good. Unclear timing (from today, or from launch?).
- **Why it hurts:** a top-down target reads as wishful thinking. A number that doesn't reconcile makes the reader distrust everything else.
- **Fix:** build bottom-up from customers, revenue per customer, retention, and growth. Show the arithmetic. State the timing basis. Fix chart scales.
- **Before you say it:** re-read the figures on the slide and run the arithmetic, as SKILL.md describes under "Verify numbers before calling a mismatch." Rule out rounding, compounding, and mismatched periods first.
- **Voice:** "Right now it reads like a top-down target." / "Something isn't adding up. Show the math."

### Hype and buzzwords over substance
- **Looks like:** stacked metaphors and taglines, buzzword feature names, repeated technology claims with no proof, hashtags, clever lines that don't connect.
- **Why it hurts:** experienced investors discount hype. Hype also raises the bar for the proof that follows: if the excitement outruns the evidence, the evidence looks weak. Think of it as the excitement-to-proof ratio.
- **Fix:** speak plainly. Pick one tagline and keep it. Put the technology claims on one slide that says what the technology actually does. Match the excitement to the evidence.
- **Voice:** "Right now it reads like hype without proof." / "Speak plainly."

### Stage of development unclear
- **Looks like:** no sign of whether the product is live, in beta, in a pilot, a prototype, or an idea waiting for money. Finished and planned work mixed together.
- **Why it hurts:** investors price risk by stage. "Everything waits until we raise" makes investors hesitate.
- **Fix:** state the stage plainly. Separate what's done from what's planned. Show what you can build before the money arrives.
- **Voice:** "It's unclear what stage you're at. Is this live? A beta? A pilot?"

## Occasional

### Visuals that confuse instead of clarify
- **Looks like:** decorative or generated images. A famous image that invites the wrong reading. A logo placed so nearby text seems to be about the company. Screenshots of other slides pasted in. Charts with floating bars or unclear axes.
- **Why it hurts:** a visual should make the point faster. If it needs interpretation, it fails.
- **Fix:** replace it with a visual that explains: a process with the broken step marked and where you step in, a diagram placing you where two needs meet, or a simple "these tools combined = us" equation. Start bars from zero.
- **Voice:** "If I need to squint or read labels, it's not working."

### The sector's known risk is missing
- **Looks like:** no mention of the risk every expert in the sector will ask about, such as the regulatory path, manufacturing scale and capital cost, licensing rights, operations and logistics, security, reimbursement, or partner margins.
- **Why it hurts:** experienced investors go straight there. Silence reads as naivety.
- **Fix:** add a slide or section naming the risk and how you handle it.
- **Voice:** "This is a key concern for investors. Address it clearly."

### Core slides missing entirely
- **Looks like:** no ask, team, traction, business model, go-to-market, or why now.
- **Fix:** list what's missing and propose an outline. See "A standard outline" in [slide checklists](slide-checklists.md).
- **Voice:** "Several core pieces are missing from the deck."

### Dates and events that age the deck
- **Looks like:** event names, presentation dates, and locations on slides. Old "as of" dates.
- **Fix:** remove them, and update stale figures.
- **Voice:** "Dates age quickly."

### Signals that don't look venture-scale
- **Looks like:** slow growth targets, an exit at small revenue, terms that imply heavy dilution, conflicting statements about runway and future raises.
- **When it applies:** only when the deck is pitched to venture investors. For a friends-and-family round, a small angel round, or project financing, a modest outcome may be the honest plan. There, check that the raise and the promised return fit the audience, and still flag conflicting statements.
- **Why it hurts:** these make the company read as a modest-growth business rather than a venture bet, and conflicting statements undermine trust.
- **Fix:** reconcile the numbers, and reconsider what the growth and exit plan signal. Drop an exit slide at an early stage. Flag obvious red flags in terms without giving valuation or term advice.
- **Voice:** "These points conflict. Tighten the narrative."

### Name, tagline, or brand risks
- **Looks like:** a name unrelated to the category or too generic to own, or a tagline with an unintended association.
- **Fix:** flag it gently, note the tradeoff, and let the founder decide.
- **Voice:** "This may sound odd, but..."
